⚖️ Executive Summary

Two teams built satellite collision-avoidance systems for FAR AWAY 2026. SpaceATC (alpha) built a multi-agent negotiation system — two AI "operator agents" bid delta-v to avoid a collision, a coordinator picks the winner, and a human approves. AstroMesh (beta) built a distributed consensus system — a Go cluster votes on maneuvers, verified with formal math (TLA+), displayed on a realistic CesiumJS 3D globe.

Panel verdict: SpaceATC wins 14 of 20 judge votes. AstroMesh has a more visually polished frontend and more realistic data pipeline, but the live demo is the strongest card at a hackathon — and SpaceATC is the only one that has it.

Glossary (plain English)

SGP4
A mathematical model that predicts where a satellite will be at any time from tracking data (TLEs). Think of it as the GPS prediction algorithm for satellites.
TLE
Two Line Element — a format encoding a satellite's orbit position velocity inclination as two lines of text. Thousands are published daily by the US Space Force.
Conjunction
When two space objects pass close enough to warrant concern — a "near miss" in orbit.
Pc (Collision Probability)
Probability two objects will collide, computed from positions and uncertainties. 1e-6 (one in a million) is a common action threshold.
Delta-V
A measure of how much a satellite must change velocity to avoid a collision. Higher = more fuel.
Clohessy-Wiltshire (CW)
A simplified math model of how two nearby satellites move relative to each other, used to plan avoidance maneuvers.
LangGraph
An AI framework (by the makers of LangChain) for building multi-step agent workflows that can pause, get human input, and resume.
TLA+
A formal mathematical language for proving that a system's design is correct — like mathematically proving a traffic light can never show green in all directions.
Bully Election
A distributed algorithm where the highest-ID node becomes the leader. Like the oldest kid deciding what game to play.
2-Phase Commit (2PC)
A protocol where multiple computers agree on a decision: first "prepare" (can everyone do it?), then "commit" (do it).
Covariance
A mathematical measure of uncertainty in a satellite's position. Like the "error bar" around a weather forecast.
HITL (Human-in-the-loop)
A system design where a human must approve before an automated decision executes.
Space-Track
The US Space Force's public catalogue of all tracked objects in orbit (~31,000).
CelesTrak
A public service that republishes Space-Track data plus the SOCRATES conjunction reports.
CDM (Conjunction Data Message)
A standardized format operators exchange when a close approach is predicted.
SOCRATES
CelesTrak's automated system screening the entire satellite catalogue daily for close approaches.
Kessler Syndrome
A runaway chain reaction where space debris creates more debris, making orbits unusable for generations.
RAG (Retrieval-Augmented Generation)
An AI pattern grounding an LLM's answer in a specific document lookup, reducing hallucinations.
YOLO (You Only Look Once)
A real-time neural network that finds objects in images or video frames.
PnP (Perspective-n-Point) Pose
A computer vision method determining a camera's position relative to a known 3D object.

🧮 Every Equation in Both Projects, in Plain English

Written for a judge who has never seen an orbital-mechanics formula. Each block shows the real formula taken from the real source file, then what it actually means. Nothing here is paraphrased from a README — every line was read out of the code.

The problem, before any math

Two objects in orbit are moving at roughly 7.6 km per second each. If they are heading toward each other they close at up to ~14 km/s — about 40× rifle-bullet speed. Nobody can steer in real time at that speed, so everything must be predicted hours ahead. Both projects answer three questions: (1) when do they come closest? (2) how bad is it? (3) how small a nudge fixes it? They answer each one differently.

Question 1 — When do they come closest? (Time of Closest Approach, "TCA")

TCA = argmin t ∥ r₁(t) − r₂(t) ∥ SpaceATC. Read it as: "walk time forward, and find the moment where the gap between the two satellites is smallest." r₁(t) and r₂(t) are the two positions at time t; the double bars just mean "the distance between them." The code does it in two passes: a coarse pass stepping through time on a grid, then scipy.optimize.minimize_scalar zooming in on the winning window to find the exact second. This is a standard and correct way to do it. backend/orbital/conjunction.py:150–175
apogee/perigee sieve → 300 s coarse sweep → fine TCA refine AstroMesh. Same goal, bigger scope. Before checking any pair it throws away pairs that cannot possibly meet: if one object's lowest altitude is above the other's highest altitude, skip it — no math needed. That "sieve" is what makes it affordable to screen a full ~31,000-object catalogue instead of one hand-picked pair. Survivors get a 5-minute-step sweep, then a fine refine. This is genuinely how real screening services do it. dev/mock-gateway.js:662–780

Question 2 — How bad is it? (Probability of Collision, "Pc")

A predicted miss distance is never exact — the tracking data is fuzzy. So instead of "they miss by 800 m", you ask "given the fuzziness, what is the chance they actually touch?" That number is Pc. Operators typically act at around 1 in 10,000 to 1 in 1,000,000. The two projects compute it in genuinely different ways, and this is the sharpest technical difference between them.

Pc = (π · R² / σ²) · exp( −½ · (d / σ)² )   with σ = 1.0 km, R = 0.01 km SpaceATC. Picture a dartboard. d is how far the dart lands from the bullseye (the miss distance). R is the size of the bullseye (both satellites' combined body size, ~10 m). σ ("sigma") is how shaky the thrower's hand is — the uncertainty. The exp(−½(d/σ)²) part is the bell curve: the chance falls off very fast as the miss grows. The honest catch, which the code states in a comment: tracking data (TLEs) carries no uncertainty figure at all, so the team assumed σ = 1 km. That is a defensible typical value, but it is an assumption, and the Pc is only as good as that guess. Judges should credit the comment that admits it. backend/orbital/conjunction.py:186–197
Pcmax = HBR² / (d² · e)    σ* = d / √2 AstroMesh. Same missing-uncertainty problem, a cleverer dodge. Rather than guess σ, ask: "of every possible uncertainty, which one makes this look worst?" That worst case turns out to be σ = d/√2 (the "dilution threshold"), and plugging it in collapses the whole bell curve into one clean line: HBR² / (d² · e), where e is Euler's number (2.718). So the answer is a guaranteed upper bound rather than a guess: "it is at most this risky." This is exactly the method CelesTrak's SOCRATES service publishes, which makes it more defensible than an assumed sigma. Credit where due — this is AstroMesh's strongest single piece of math. dev/mock-gateway.js:676–689

Question 3 — How small a nudge fixes it? (Delta-V)

Delta-v is just "change in speed", measured in metres per second. It is the currency of spaceflight: every m/s spent is fuel burned, and fuel is finite and unreplaceable — spend too much and the satellite's working life gets shorter. So the goal is never "dodge hard", it is "dodge by the smallest amount that is provably safe."

along-track coefficient = ( 4 sin(nτ) − 3 nτ ) / n SpaceATC (Clohessy–Wiltshire). n is how fast the satellite sweeps around its orbit, τ is how long before the encounter you fire the thruster. The formula answers: "if I nudge my speed now, how far ahead or behind my old track will I be at the encounter?" Two useful things fall out of it. First, the leverage is enormous — nudging early moves you kilometres later for centimetres per second now, which is why avoidance manoeuvres are tiny. Second, the sign flips: with a 60-minute lead, nτ ≈ 3.95 radians, past π, so speeding up actually puts you behind where you wanted — the code correctly picks a retrograde (slow-down) burn. That sign check is a real detail most hackathon projects get wrong. The final number is then found by binary search: shrink the burn until the Pc is safe, and refuse to return anything outside 0.001–500 m/s or above Pc 1e-4. backend/orbital/conjunction.py:210–380

SpaceATC's CW formula assumes a near-circular orbit and a nearby partner — fine for the demo, wrong for stretched orbits. This equation is the general version: one formula that fast-forwards any orbit — circular, elliptical, even escape trajectories — by any amount of time. χ ("chi") is a bookkeeping variable that stands in for "how far along the orbit we went", solved by Newton's method (guess, correct, repeat, ~200 iterations max). C(z) and S(z) are the Stumpff functions, the two helper series that keep the same formula valid whether the orbit curves closed or open. Practical payoff: after planning a dodge, AstroMesh can re-fly the new orbit and re-screen it against the whole catalogue to check the fix did not create a second conjunction. SGP4
The standard formula the US Space Force publishes for turning a satellite's tracking record into "where will it be at 14:32 tomorrow." Both projects use it. Neither wrote it — both use the well-tested library version, which is the correct choice.
LangGraph (SpaceATC)

Bully election / 2-Phase Commit (AstroMesh)
Rules for a group of computers with no boss. "Bully election": whoever has the highest ID declares itself leader. "2-Phase Commit": the leader asks everyone "can you do this?", and only if all say yes does it say "do it now." Standard, well-understood distributed-systems building blocks.
TLA+ (AstroMesh)
A language for proving a design cannot misbehave, rather than testing and hoping. Think of proving a traffic light can never show green both ways, instead of watching the intersection for a week. AstroMesh ships a real 8 KB specification. Note the boundary: TLA+ proves the design, not the code that was actually written.
Hard-body radius (HBR)
Both satellites treated as spheres and added together — roughly 10 m combined. It is the size of the target you are trying not to hit.
